# RESEARCH: Game & Studio Failures — Deep Multilingual Survey (Sept 2026)

> Companion file to the origins/evolution and breakout-success research files. Self-contained —
> written to be read without the conversation that produced it. Covers commercial flops, studio
> shutdowns, dead live-service games, crowdfunding disasters, monetization backlash, platform-risk
> deaths, and burnout/collapse stories, across Western, Japanese, Korean, Chinese and Russian/CIS
> sources. Ends with a synthesis of recurring patterns and an explicit relevance check against
> Cosmogram's own shape (solo non-programmer owner + AI coding assistant, Telegram-native,
> ethics-constrained monetization via a "Charter," Telegram Stars as the only real-money channel).

## 1. AAA / big-budget live-service graveyard (Western)

The 2020s produced an unusually dense cluster of expensive live-service games that died within
months of launch. The pattern repeats so consistently it has its own vocabulary ("to Crucible
something" = to panic-unrelease a failed live service).

### Concord (Firewalk Studios / Sony, 2024)
Launched August 23, 2024 as a $40 premium hero shooter; taken offline **14 days later** on
September 6 due to a peak Steam concurrent player count under 700. Estimated development cost
$200–400M over roughly 8 years, reportedly making it the most expensive failure in PlayStation's
history. Sony shut Firewalk down entirely in October 2024, laying off 210 people. Root cause per
critics and Sony's own statement: a hero shooter with generic character/level design entering an
already saturated genre (Overwatch 2, Valorant) with nothing to differentiate it — a "me-too"
product shipped years after the genre's window had closed. *Source: GameSpot, Aftermath, TheSixthAxis retrospective (Sept 2025).*

### Babylon's Fall (PlatinumGames / Square Enix, 2022)
PlatinumGames' first live-service title. Concurrent Steam players fell under 1,200 shortly after
launch and to as low as 1 player two months in. Square Enix ended support six months after launch
and fully shut servers down February 27, 2023 — under a year of live service. Root cause: entering
live-service as a genre experiment (CEO Atsushi Inaba's personal interest) without the live-service
production discipline (content cadence, monetization, community management) the studio's prior
single-player pedigree hadn't required. *Source: GameDeveloper.com, Kotaku, Forbes (2022–23).*

### Anthem (BioWare / EA, 2019)
Not a rapid shutdown but a slow-motion one: five-plus years of development with the creative
direction rebooted multiple times, core promised features (a "living world" that reacted to
players) repeatedly scrapped, and senior staff burning out and leaving mid-project — the
so-called "BioWare Magic" failure mode, where a project is expected to coalesce at the last minute
the way earlier BioWare games had, and simply doesn't. EA later cancelled a planned overhaul
("Anthem Next"). Root cause: chronic lack of a stable creative vision combined with unsustainable
crunch used as a substitute for planning. *Source: Kotaku (Jason Schreier investigation), Inverse retrospective.*

### Redfall / Arkane Austin (Microsoft, 2023–2024)
Redfall was a critical and commercial flop in 2023; Microsoft closed Arkane Austin in May 2024
alongside Tango Gameworks (creator of the well-reviewed Hi-Fi Rush) and Alpha Dog Games. Reporting
indicated studio leadership's push into live-service design (a genre the studio had no prior
technical or design experience in) drove high internal turnover well before launch. Notably,
Arkane Austin used its final months to ship the offline mode and pause feature players had asked
for since launch — polish arriving only after the studio's fate was already sealed. Root cause:
a single-player-specialist studio redirected into an unfamiliar genre under external mandate, with
turnover compounding the execution risk. *Source: PCGamer, GameDeveloper.com, KVUE (2024).*

### Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League (Rocksteady / WB Games, 2024)
Seven-plus years of development; reported ~$200M in write-offs for Warner Bros. Rocksteady — a
studio built entirely on single-player Arkham games — was pushed (by its own account, not
externally mandated the way some reports first suggested) into a live-service genre pivot midway
through development, with a "constantly shifting vision" and "a culture of rigid perfectionism"
compounding the mismatch. Designers described the late stage of development as "following a
spreadsheet" rather than making a game, and some leads considered leaving the industry entirely
after the launch's failure. *Source: GamesRadar+, GameDeveloper.com, Kotaku (2024).*

### The hero-shooter graveyard (general pattern, 2016–2020)
Battleborn (Gearbox), LawBreakers (Cliff Bleszinski/Boss Key), Radical Heights, and Evolve
(Turtle Rock) are the canonical earlier cluster of this same failure: well-funded competitive
multiplayer games launched into a market a single dominant title (Overwatch, then Fortnite) had
already claimed, with none offering a decisive enough reason to switch. Boss Key Productions
closed outright after LawBreakers and Radical Heights both failed to find an audience.

### Amazon Games' MMO retreat (Crucible, New World, 2020–2026)
Crucible launched May 20, 2020, was pulled back to closed beta after **31 days** (July 2020) as
"to Crucible something" entered industry slang, and was cancelled outright three months later —
one of the fastest total reversals of a shipped live-service game on record. New World launched
stronger (900k+ concurrent peak, Sept 2021) but declined steadily; a 2024 console relaunch
("Aeternum") attempted an FFXIV-style comeback and failed to reverse the trend — New World is
scheduled to go fully offline January 31, 2027. By August 2026 Amazon had handed back its two
licensed MMOs (Lost Ark, Throne and Liberty) to their original Korean developers, effectively
exiting the MMO genre after roughly a decade and multiple failed attempts. Root cause across all
three: a company with immense infrastructure and marketing resources but no accumulated live-service
game-design judgment, repeatedly re-learning the same lessons. *Source: GameSpot, Forbes, lemonlama.net retrospective (2025–26).*

### XDefiant (Ubisoft, 2024–2025)
Launched May 2024, announced shutdown roughly six months later (Dec 2024), fully closed June 2025.
Cited causes: development/hosting cost exceeding realistic revenue, weak monetization uptake, and
a self-inflicted distribution error — Ubisoft restricted the free-to-play shooter to its own
launcher rather than Steam, a platform-choice mistake widely blamed for capping its reachable
audience from day one. Studio closures in San Francisco and Osaka followed (143 + 134 jobs).
*Source: ScreenRant, GameDeveloper.com, Variety (2024).*

### Google Stadia (Google, 2019–2023)
Not a single game but a platform-level failure directly relevant to any project dependent on a
third-party platform. Killed January 18, 2023. Root causes per multiple postmortems: no content
strategy (Google closed its own first-party Stadia studios in Feb 2021, before a single first-party
game shipped), confusing pricing (full game price plus a subscription, no ownership), weak
marketing/awareness, and — most structurally — a total organizational mismatch: a live game
platform needs publishing/operations/partner-first patience that a metrics-driven ad company
doesn't reward internally. Stadia is one of 270+ discontinued Google products; the pattern of
killing underperforming products fast is a corporate habit that made a commitment-dependent product
category (a game platform players must trust with purchases) structurally unsuited to that company.
*Source: Forbes, TechRadar, MIDiA Research "The fall of Stadia" (2023).*

## 2. Financial collapse & studio implosion

### 38 Studios / Kingdoms of Amalur (USA, 2012)
Founded by former baseball pitcher Curt Schilling; the state of Rhode Island loaned the studio
$75M in economic-development bonds to relocate there. The studio shipped one well-reviewed game
(Kingdoms of Amalur: Reckoning) and filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy months later, with debts over
$150M. Cause: the game's decent reviews and sales could never have serviced a burn rate reportedly
in the millions per month, driven by high-end perks and a payroll built for a much bigger hit than
one mid-tier RPG could fund — a mismatch between capital raised (much of it public money, tied to
political promises rather than market judgment) and realistic revenue. Former employees only
received their final owed paychecks in **2021, nine years later**. *Source: TheGamer, Slate, PCGamer (2012–2021).*

### The Day Before / Fntastic (Russia-founded, global remote team, 2023)
The starkest recent example of hype without substance. Marketed for years as an ambitious
zombie-survival MMO built largely by unpaid volunteer labor; became Steam's most-wishlisted game
on the strength of trailers alone. Shipped December 7, 2023 as a comparatively empty, buggy
extraction shooter — nothing like the MMO that had been marketed. Peaked at ~38,000 concurrent
players, received overwhelmingly negative reviews, and the studio **closed 4 days later**, stating
plainly: "The Day Before has failed financially, and we lack the funds to continue... we
miscalculated our possibilities." Root cause: a studio whose actual production capability never
matched its marketing, financed on the assumption that launch revenue itself would resolve
pre-existing debt — a bet that failed the moment reviews turned negative. *Source: PCGamer, Digital Trends, GameDeveloper.com, Kotaku (Dec 2023).*

### Embracer Group breakup (Sweden, 2023–2024)
A cautionary tale about growth-by-acquisition without integration discipline. A $2 billion deal
with Saudi-backed Savvy Games collapsed in 2023, taking 40% off Embracer's share price and leaving
it heavily indebted. Consequences over the following year: 44 studios closed, roughly 80
in-development projects cancelled (including a new Deus Ex and the TimeSplitters reboot from
resurrected studio Free Radical Design — cancelled *before* release despite years of visible
development), and workforce cut from ~15,700 to ~7,900 (nearly 7,800 jobs lost). Embracer
ultimately split into three independent public companies in 2024. Root cause: a holding company
that had spent years acquiring studios faster than it could fund or manage their output, betting
on a single large external deal to paper over the gap — when that deal fell through, the exposure
was structural, not one bad game. *Source: GameDeveloper.com, GGRecon, PureXbox, Udonis (2023–24).*

## 3. Crowdfunding disasters

### Yogventures! (UK, Winterkewl Games, 2012–2014)
Raised $567K from 13,647 Kickstarter backers for a Minecraft-like sandbox, backed by the Yogscast
YouTube brand's audience trust. A six-person team proved unable to deliver a project of that scope;
it was cancelled in 2014 with **no refunds** — backers were offered a copy of a different,
unrelated crowdfunded game as a consolation. The founder reportedly invested $25K of his own money
and, by his own account, his marriage, trying to save it. Root cause: scope far beyond a small
team's actual capacity, sold on the strength of an influencer audience's trust rather than a
production plan. *Source: PCGamer, Forbes (2014).*

### Mighty No. 9 (Japan/USA, Comcept, 2013–2016)
Successfully funded in two days (eventually 400%+ of its Kickstarter goal), riding nostalgia for
Mega Man creator Keiji Inafune. Three years of development followed, marked by poor backer
communication, visible graphical downgrades between successive footage reveals, and repeated
delays — while Comcept launched *another* Kickstarter campaign before this one shipped, which
backers read (reasonably) as a signal of financial mismanagement. Released to mixed-to-negative
reviews. Root cause: crowdfunding trust spent faster than it was replenished by visible progress;
transparency deficit compounded technical/design problems rather than covering for them.
*Source: Wikipedia, Forbes (2016).*

### Star Citizen (Cloud Imperium Games, 2012–ongoing)
Included here as the canonical scope-creep cautionary tale, not a "failure" in the shut-down
sense — it is still funded and still shipping updates as of 2026, having crossed **$1 billion**
in cumulative crowdfunding after 14 years without a 1.0 release. The 2012 pitch was a space combat
sim; by 2026 the promised feature set included dynamic server meshing, procedural cities, full
persistence, base building, salvage, medical gameplay, and a separately-studio'd single-player
campaign (Squadron 42) that itself ballooned from a planned tutorial into a stand-alone production.
Analysts note the business model may structurally reward *not* finishing, since a shipped 1.0 ends
the perpetual pre-order funding stream that sustains it. Root cause / lesson: crowdfunding removes
the natural external pressure (a publisher's release deadline, a bank loan's repayment schedule)
that normally forces scope discipline — without a self-imposed substitute, scope grows to fill
whatever funding keeps arriving. *Source: GameRant, lemonlama.net, PlayerAuctions (2026).*

## 4. Monetization backlash — even against playable, well-liked games

This category matters most for a project with an explicit anti-predatory-monetization Charter:
it shows both that monetization overreach genuinely destroys goodwill even in successful games,
*and* that regulatory/legal consequences can follow specific mechanics years later.

### Star Wars Battlefront II loot boxes (EA, 2017)
At launch, iconic characters (Luke Skywalker, Darth Vader) were locked behind grinding or
loot-box-funded random unlocks in a $60 game. The backlash (a since-deleted EA reddit reply
explaining the system became, at the time, the most downvoted comment in Reddit history) forced
EA to pull real-money loot box purchases within days and rework progression to cosmetics-only.
Belgium's and the Netherlands' gambling regulators opened formal investigations into whether
loot boxes constituted gambling; **both countries banned paid loot boxes with randomized-odds
mechanics outright in 2018**, a regulatory response traceable directly to this one launch.
Root cause: monetization design treated as separate from game design, discovered by players in
the worst possible way (locking beloved characters), at full premium price. *Source: Engadget, Gizmodo, GBC-Time (2017).*

### Diablo Immortal (Blizzard, 2022)
A well-reviewed action-RPG (67 Metacritic critic score) whose monetization design — a "hidden
whale mechanic" in its gem-upgrade system — was calculated by players to cost somewhere between
$50,000 and $540,000 (estimates varied and were later revised down after Blizzard adjusted rates)
to fully max a character. Player review score cratered to 0.2/10 on PC, at the time the lowest
user score in Metacritic's history — driven entirely by monetization backlash, not gameplay
quality. The game still made over $40M in its first week and remained commercially successful
despite the backlash, which is itself an important, uncomfortable data point: reputational damage
and revenue are not the same axis, and a studio can "win" financially while visibly damaging trust.
*Source: WBUR, GameWorldObserver, Forbes (2022).*

### Escape from Tarkov "Unheard Edition" (Battlestate Games, Russia, 2024)
A live example of monetization creep breaking a promise made to existing paying customers. The
previous top-tier "Edge of Darkness" edition had been sold with a stated promise of free access
to all future DLC. In 2024, Battlestate discontinued that edition and introduced a new $250
"Unheard Edition" that (a) required existing EoD owners to pay again for a promised-free feature,
and (b) bundled clearly pay-to-win perks (discounted in-raid services, faster insurance returns,
AI behavior changes favoring paying players). Backlash was severe enough that CEO Nikita Buyanov
personally addressed it days later, and the company partially reversed course (giving EoD owners
six months of free access to the new mode before opening it to all). Root cause: monetization
decisions that retroactively broke an explicit prior promise to the game's most loyal, highest-LTV
customers — trust violations land hardest exactly where the relationship was previously strongest.
*Source: GameSpot, PCGamesN, Forbes (2024).*

### Wemade / MIR4 and Korea's play-to-earn ban (Korea, 2021–2023)
MIR4's global version added blockchain "play-to-earn" mechanics tied to a cryptocurrency
(WEMIX/DRACO), driving a large international audience. Korea's game-classification authority
(Game Rating and Administration Committee) has consistently refused to rate P2E games for
domestic release on the grounds that convertible in-game currency constitutes gambling under
Korean law; a 2023 court ruling upheld the ban. Result: Wemade, a publicly listed Korean company,
had to route its P2E strategy entirely around its home market — building global-only products
while remaining functionally illegal in the country it's headquartered in, alongside a public
government contradiction (a state content-fund program funded a P2E developer the games regulator
simultaneously classified as illegal). Root cause / lesson: crypto-tied monetization can run into
outright legal prohibition, not just community backlash — and that risk is jurisdiction-specific
and can arrive well after a mechanic has already shipped and scaled. *Source: Lexology, KCI, Newsis, Seoul Shinmun (2021–2023).*

## 5. Platform / policy risk — failures the developer didn't cause

This is the category most structurally relevant to a Telegram-native project: games that died not
because they were bad, but because the platform underneath them changed the rules.

### Adobe Flash end-of-life (global, announced 2017, executed Dec 31 2020)
Major browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge) coordinated to disable the Flash plugin by end of
2020 following Adobe's own announced sunset. Thousands of browser games built in Flash — an entire
generation of web gaming (Kongregate, Newgrounds-era content, countless Facebook games) — became
unplayable literally overnight, with no native way to open them, because they depended on a
third-party runtime the developer didn't control and couldn't extend past its vendor's decision.
Some content was later revived by third parties porting it to HTML5 (Poki and others), but the
vast majority of Flash-era games were simply lost. Root cause: building on a proprietary runtime
whose continued existence depended entirely on one company's roadmap, with no migration plan in
place before the deadline was announced years in advance (yet many studios still didn't act).
*Source: EM360Tech, FastCompany, Qualys (2020–21).*

### FarmVille / Zynga (2020)
FarmVille's shutdown (Dec 31, 2020) is usually told as "Flash died and took it with it" — accurate
as the proximate trigger — but the game had already been declining for years before that: the
2011–2012 shift to mobile casual gaming and repeated changes to Facebook's platform algorithm and
policies had already eroded its audience well before the technical kill switch arrived. Root cause:
over-dependence on a single social platform's distribution mechanics (Facebook's news-feed virality,
which Facebook itself throttled over time) compounded by a second single point of failure (Flash),
so that two separate platform decisions outside Zynga's control both eventually mattered.
*Source: TheWrap, GameDeveloper.com, ProductMint (2020).*

### Marvel Heroes / Gazillion Entertainment (2017)
Disney/Marvel terminated its license with developer Gazillion in November 2017, and the MMO shut
down within days. The popular telling is "Disney pulled the plug" — but reporting from former
employees suggests the deeper cause was that Gazillion had no other revenue source, was deep in
debt to investors, banks, and its own employees, and had effectively become a cash drain Disney
had no further reason to prop up; the license revocation was the final domino, not the sole cause.
Employees were laid off with no severance or PTO. Root cause: total dependence on a single
licensor's goodwill, with the studio's own finances already unsustainable independent of that
relationship — the license pull was the visible trigger, financial fragility was the real cause.
*Source: GameInformer, MassivelyOP, Kotaku (2017).*

### App Tracking Transparency / IDFA and the hypercasual crash (2021–2022)
Apple's 2021 App Tracking Transparency policy deprecated the IDFA advertiser identifier hypercasual
mobile studios depended on for precise, cheap user-acquisition targeting. Reported iOS revenue
drops of 15–20% for many titles followed, and the entire hypercasual business model — built on
extremely thin per-user margins that only worked with very precise, very cheap ad targeting —
became structurally less viable almost overnight. Surviving studios like Voodoo pivoted to
"hybridcasual" (adding light meta-progression/retention systems) and cut release cadence sharply
(to as few as four titles/year from a much higher historical rate). Root cause: a business model
whose unit economics depended entirely on one platform's ad-tracking permissiveness, with no
contingency built in before the policy shipped (Apple announced it well in advance, but the
economics of pivoting quickly enough were still brutal for smaller studios). *Source: MobileDevMemo, PocketGamer.biz, Deconstructor of Fun (2021–22).*

### Hamster Kombat & the Telegram crypto-game crash (Telegram-native, 2024–2025)
Directly analogous to Cosmogram's own platform. Hamster Kombat, a Telegram Mini App "tap-to-earn"
crypto game, grew to roughly 300 million players by mid-2024 on the promise of a future token
airdrop. When the HMSTR token actually launched on TON in September 2024, its price fell over 50%
within days, and many eligible players received allocations far smaller than expected ("dust"),
triggering intense community backlash over perceived unfairness in the distribution formula. The
token later fell to an all-time low; the game lost roughly 85% of its peak player base within
months, down to ~12 million monthly players from a 300 million high. Root cause: a player base
recruited almost entirely by the promise of free money rather than by the game itself had no
reason to stay once that promise under-delivered — the "game" content was never the retention
mechanism, so once the financial hook broke, essentially all engagement broke with it. This is the
single most directly relevant case study for any Telegram-native monetization decision: **a
Telegram audience acquired through a real-money promise evaporates the moment that promise
disappoints, almost regardless of the underlying game quality.** *Source: Decrypt, Bitcoinist, DailyCoin, CCN (2024–25).*

## 6. Korean & Chinese MMO shutdowns (under-documented in English — searched natively)

### 야생의 땅: 듀랑고 (Durango: Wild Lands) — Nexon, Korea, 2018–2019
A critically well-regarded, genuinely original open-world survival MMO (dinosaurs, dynamic
ecosystem) that nonetheless shut down after not even two full years of live service (service ended
Dec 18, 2019). Nexon's own public statement was notably vague — a "business decision" made as part
of a broader "selection and concentration" strategy to focus resources elsewhere, explicitly *not*
attributing the closure to any single design or monetization failure. This is itself instructive:
even a large, well-resourced publisher will kill an original, acclaimed game quietly for portfolio-
prioritization reasons that have little to do with the game's own merits — "the game wasn't bad"
is not protection against shutdown when a parent company reallocates resources. *Source: Gametoc, ZDNet Korea, Gamemeca (Korean, 2019).*

### 블레스 (Bless Online) — Neowiz, Korea, 2016–2019
Launched to strong initial buzz (reached #4 in Korean online-game popularity rankings) after a
reported ~70 billion KRW (~$60M+) development budget, but underwent a large forced server merge
just one month after launch — an early, visible signal of underperformance — and shut down roughly
two years later. Root causes identified by Korean coverage: an outdated tab-targeting combat
system that didn't match contemporary market expectations, and a broader lack of player-facing
convenience/customization (no keybinding options, no UI customization) read as basic neglect of
player comfort. Root cause: a large, expensive, technically competent MMO that simply didn't
modernize its core feel relative to what the market had already moved past by launch. *Source: Gamemeca, Sisaweek, Inven (Korean, 2016–2019).*

### China's 2024 mass live-service shutdown wave (Tencent / NetEase / Bilibili)
Chinese industry coverage documents 2024 as a "断舍离" ("cut ties, let go") year: 114 games ceased
operation in 2024 alone, with over 20% being "二次元" (anime-style gacha) titles, and the shortest-
lived title shutting down before even completing one month of service. Named examples: Tencent's
《食物语》(a female-oriented RPG that launched in 2019 and lost ground as the genre got crowded)
and 《白夜极光》(well-reviewed for art quality but underperformed financially despite the praise —
critical acclaim without matching revenue); NetEase shut down three titles in a single day in
November 2024 (《机动都市阿尔法》《战争怒吼》《泰亚史诗》) plus its Marvel-licensed 《漫威超级战争》
earlier that year; Bilibili shut down 《机动战姬：聚变》despite it having reached #12 on the
bestseller chart at its peak. Common thread across nearly every case cited by Chinese trade press:
"不及预期的流水和收入" (revenue below internal projections) — not necessarily bad reviews, bad
player numbers in absolute terms, or bad games, but revenue that didn't clear an internal bar set
by a large conglomerate managing many products at once. Root cause / lesson: at large-publisher
scale, a live-service game doesn't need to fail on its own terms to be killed — it just needs to
underperform relative to the publisher's *other* opportunities for the same engineering headcount.
*Source: 每经网 (NBD), 游戏日报, 腾讯新闻 (Chinese, Dec 2024–Sept 2025).*

## 7. Japanese industry — contraction, gacha churn, and one major recovery

### Final Fantasy XIV 1.0 (Square Enix, 2010) — the canonical failure-then-recovery
FFXIV's original 2010 release drew immediate, intense criticism over its UI and control
responsiveness, and players left rapidly enough that Square Enix's own president publicly
apologized and announced a full production reboot. Director/producer Naoki Yoshida (brought in
after the failure, not present for the original launch) later explained the root cause candidly:
the prior team's *success* with Final Fantasy XI had made them overconfident — "a very large
success experience is, conversely, dangerous" — and the original 1.0 team judged it faster and
safer to patch a fundamentally under-built game than to admit it needed a ground-up rebuild.
Yoshida concluded the opposite after months of assessment: a full rebuild (graphics, UI, story)
was the only real fix, releasing "A Realm Reborn" in August 2013. This is one of the only cases in
this survey of a AAA-scale launch failure being **fully reversed** into a long-term commercial
success — and the reversal required publicly admitting the original was unsalvageable rather than
iterating on it. *Source: 4Gamer, Famitsu, GAME Watch, The社史 (Japanese, various).*

### Japanese mobile gacha short-lifecycle churn (ongoing pattern)
Japanese trade press describes a structural, ongoing pattern rather than isolated incidents:
sōshage (ソシャゲ, social/mobile games) frequently derive 80%+ of revenue from gacha pulls, and
players report "課金疲れ" (monetization fatigue) at scale; IP-licensed games based on anime/manga
are specifically noted to be *designed* for short lifespans — a licensor deal and low development
budget mean the game can recoup costs and profit even from a short run, so short-lived-by-design is
a viable, common business strategy rather than always a failure mode. Bankruptcies among mobile
game companies were reported running at a record pace as of 2026. One concrete recent example
cited: 文豪ストレイドッグス 迷ヰ犬怪奇譚 shut down in July 2026 when its operating company,
Ambition, ceased business entirely for financial reasons — the game's own performance was
secondary to the publisher's survival. Root cause / lesson: in gacha-heavy markets, "short service
life" is sometimes the intended business model, not a failure signal — the distinction matters
when assessing what "success" should even mean for a small live-service product. *Source: Yahoo!ニュース (ピンズバNEWS), note.com, 神ゲー攻略 (Japanese, 2026).*

### Square Enix Western restructuring (2024–2025)
Following a 2023 leadership change, Square Enix announced a "multiplatform" strategic pivot in
2024, then in 2025 executed large-scale layoffs across nearly all Western (US/EU) publishing
functions — IT, marketing, publishing, sales, QA, business planning — while pulling development
resources back toward Japan-based studios; over 100 Western jobs affected, with the company's
indie-publishing arm (Square Enix Collective) among the casualties. Root cause: a large legacy
publisher correcting a prior bet on Western AAA expansion that hadn't delivered proportionate
returns, choosing geographic concentration over geographic diversification as the fix. *Source: GameBusiness.jp, ワカリタイムズ, GAME TRONIKA (Japanese, 2024–2025).*

## 8. Russian / CIS studio struggles

### Mass relocation of Russian game companies (2022 onward)
Following Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine and the resulting sanctions, several notable Russian
mobile/game companies (Plarium, Game Insight among the named examples) relocated operations out of
Russia entirely, taking key staff with them — reported in Russian tech press as companies
"вывозят айтишников семьями" (relocating IT staff with their families). Simultaneously, major
Western publishers cut off the Russian market from the other direction: Activision Blizzard
suspended Russian operations March 5, 2022; Bethesda blocked Russian purchases on Steam and Epic
March 7, 2022. Remaining Russia-based developers faced (and still face) restricted access to
paid software subscriptions and international payment rails. Root cause: geopolitical/sanctions
risk that is entirely exogenous to game quality or business execution — a risk category no amount
of good design or monetization discipline can hedge against, only relocation or market
diversification can. *Source: BFM.ru, dev.by, news.tek.fm, mmo13.ru (Russian, 2022).*

### GSC Game World / S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 (Ukraine, 2022–2024)
The studio, based in Kyiv, partially relocated to Prague after the 2022 invasion, evacuating staff
and families to western Ukraine and Hungary before re-establishing in Czechia. Not all staff were
able or willing to leave — some continued contributing to development from within Ukraine, working
from improvised spaces (including bathrooms, per developer accounts) or taking up arms to fight
instead. The Prague office later suffered a fire (Sept 2024) that heavily damaged one of three
floors, and the studio separately reported sustained Russian-linked hacking attempts leaking
internal test builds over roughly a year and a half. Despite all of this, the game *did* ship
(Nov 2024) — making this less a "failure" case and more an extreme illustration of how much
disruption a small/mid studio can absorb and still deliver, at enormous human cost. Relevant as a
counter-example: catastrophic external disruption does not automatically mean project death if the
team's commitment survives it. *Source: NME, VGC, Inven Global, TechRadar (2022–2024).*

### Atomic Heart / Mundfish boycott (2023)
A commercially successful game (unlike most entries in this file) included here for the reputational
mechanism, not financial failure. Mundfish, developing from apparently Russian origins, faced a
sustained boycott campaign over: opacity about its Russian ties and business addresses; a Russian
distribution deal via VK Play (VK being roughly half-owned by Gazprom, a company with close
Russian-state ties); a leaked privacy policy clause stating user data "may [be] provide[d] to
Russian state authorities"; and — compounding all of the above — a deliberately vague public
stance on the war that read to many as evasive rather than neutral, including reports of blocking
users who pressed for clarity. Mundfish relocated to Croatia and made some conciliatory gestures
(a composer donating fees to Ukraine aid) but never resolved the core trust deficit. Root cause /
lesson: in a geopolitically charged category, *ambiguity itself* becomes the reputational risk —
silence or vagueness on a politically loaded question is read as a stance, not as neutrality, and
blocking critics compounds rather than defuses the perception. *Source: Den of Geek, Dexerto, Vice, Gamepur (2023).*

### Russian indie burnout and closures (ongoing)
Russian trade press (DTF, Novaya Gazeta Europe) documents a broader post-2022 collapse in Russian
indie funding: Russian-speaking studios reportedly struggled to secure funding even from
Russian-language investment funds after 2022, and by some estimates more than half of small
Russian indie projects active before the war had stopped operating by 2026. First-person developer
accounts describe familiar small-studio failure mechanics independent of geopolitics — one
documented case involved a founder running out of money to pay a team, watching development stall
as unpaid staff stopped working, receiving repeated "money is coming" assurances from partners
that never materialized, and eventually being forced into an ultimatum (pay debts within a week or
close). Root cause pattern (general, not Russia-specific): betting an entire studio on a single
unshipped project with insufficient runway, compounded here by a funding environment that had
become specifically hostile to Russian-origin teams regardless of project quality. *Source: DTF.ru, Novaya Gazeta Europe (Russian, 2023–2026).*

## 9. Solo-developer and small-team collapse under sudden growth or backlash

Highly relevant given Cosmogram's own explicit scale-risk concerns (see `.knowledge/` decision
registry on the "риски масштаба" topic) — these are cases where success itself, or the public
reaction to a creator, is what broke the project, not a design or business failure.

### Flappy Bird / Dong Nguyen (Vietnam, 2014)
A single-developer hit that briefly earned an estimated $50K/day, pulled from app stores by its
creator in February 2014 at the absolute peak of its popularity — not because of any external
mandate, but because of the creator's own guilt: Nguyen, a self-described introvert with a history
of concern about his own gaming habits, felt the game's addictiveness was doing net harm to
players, and simultaneously found the sudden fame unbearable — sleep deprivation, anxiety, death
threats, racist harassment, and being unable to walk in his own neighborhood without being
accosted. He took the game down himself and stepped away from both his day job and public life.
Root cause: a solo creator with no team, PR support, or personal infrastructure to absorb sudden
global attention had no buffer between "made something people love" and "personal life destroyed
by it" — the growth itself was the crisis, independent of the product's quality or any business
mistake. *Source: GameDeveloper.com, Forbes, TheRunway.ventures (2014).*

### Fez II / Phil Fish (Canada, 2013)
A successful, acclaimed indie developer (Fez, 2012) publicly cancelled his highly anticipated
sequel and announced he was quitting the games industry, entirely in the course of a single Twitter
argument with a games journalist — triggered by an unrelated on-air comment, escalated by Fish
publicly telling the journalist to commit suicide, and culminating (the same day) with Fish being
doxxed on 4chan in apparent retaliation for his public support of another developer targeted by
early Gamergate harassment. Root cause: an already fragile public-facing solo/small-team creator,
operating with no institutional buffer (PR team, moderation support, legal/security response) around
online harassment, had a single bad day become an irreversible business decision — the cancellation
was made in the heat of the moment and never reversed. Directly relevant lesson for any small team
whose public-facing decisions are made by one exposed individual: reputational/harassment risk can
convert into permanent project-ending decisions faster than any design or funding problem can.
*Source: Kotaku, Know Your Meme, NBC News (2013).*

## 10. Indiepocalypse / discoverability collapse (Steam & mobile storefronts)

The "indiepocalypse" framing (coined roughly 2013–2014, still current) argues the core problem was
never that too many games exist in absolute terms, but that **discovery**, not creation, is the
bottleneck: over 20,000 games launched on Steam in 2025 alone (more than double 2020's ~9,654), and
by industry analysis 66% of games released on Steam in the past year got fewer than 10,000
wishlists, with 29% failing to reach even 1,000. Two additional structural pressures specific to
2025–2026 worsen this further: (1) roughly 8,000 Steam games flagged AI-generated content in just
H1 2025 (vs ~1,000 for all of 2024, an eightfold jump), diluting buyer trust in the storefront's
signal quality broadly; and (2) TikTok's organic reach collapse in early 2025 removed what had
become the single most effective free discovery channel small developers relied on to substitute
for paid marketing budgets they didn't have. Chinese industry commentary (知乎, GameRes) frames the
same underlying dynamic locally: independent development is "九死一生" (nine deaths, one survival);
even among 2019's crop of Chinese indie releases, only one title (《隐形守护者》) sold more than
500,000 copies, and most releases can't even recoup development cost. Root cause common to both
markets: a fixed or shrinking pool of player attention divided across a rapidly growing supply of
releases, with free/organic discovery channels themselves becoming less reliable over time — making
"we made a good game" progressively less sufficient on its own, regardless of region.
*Source: TheGamer, Outlook Respawn, thegamingfoundry.com (2025–26); 知乎, GameRes.com, 游民星空 (Chinese, various).*

## 11. Synthesis — recurring patterns across every era and region

Reading across roughly 30 cases spanning 2010–2026 and five language/region clusters, the same
handful of root-cause shapes recur, almost independent of budget size, era, or country:

1. **Chasing someone else's proven formula without the underlying capability.** Concord, Babylon's
   Fall, Redfall, Suicide Squad, and the entire 2016–2020 hero-shooter graveyard are all
   single-genre-specialist studios (or first-time entrants) copying a competitor's live-service
   success pattern without that competitor's years of accumulated live-ops muscle, hitting the
   market years after the genre's real opportunity window had closed.

2. **Scope grows to fill whatever funding or attention keeps arriving, absent an external forcing
   function.** Star Citizen, Yogventures, Mighty No. 9, Anthem's repeated reboots — all show that
   the normal disciplining forces (a publisher deadline, a bank's repayment schedule, market
   pressure) that keep scope in check are exactly what crowdfunding, over-funding, or "BioWare
   Magic"-style faith in eventual coalescence remove.

3. **Trust, once spent, doesn't come back cheap — and it breaks fastest exactly where it was
   strongest.** Battlefront II, Diablo Immortal, and Tarkov's Unheard Edition all show that
   monetization backlash lands hardest against a game's *most loyal, highest-spending* existing
   customers when a promise to them specifically is broken — not against new or casual players.

4. **Platform dependency is an unhedgeable risk category, not a design problem.** Flash's
   discontinuation, Facebook's algorithm changes, Apple's ATT/IDFA policy, Disney pulling a
   license, and Telegram's own tap-to-earn crypto-game boom-bust cycle (Hamster Kombat) are all
   cases where the actual game, its design quality, and its team's execution were nearly
   irrelevant to the outcome — a platform owner made a decision for its own reasons, and every
   product built on top absorbed the consequences with zero warning window that mattered in
   practice (even when the policy itself was announced far in advance, as with Flash and ATT).

5. **A conglomerate can kill a good, even acclaimed, product purely on relative-opportunity-cost
   grounds.** Durango, 白夜极光, and dozens of the 2024 Chinese shutdowns were not failures on
   their own terms (positive reviews, respectable player numbers) — they were reallocations of a
   large company's finite engineering headcount toward something with a better return, where "not
   bad" simply wasn't good enough relative to the alternative use of the same people.

6. **Geopolitical/regulatory risk is real, sudden, and asymmetric to game quality.** Sanctions-
   driven exits (Russian studios post-2022), legal prohibition of a monetization mechanic
   (Korea's P2E ban), and boycotts driven by perceived political ambiguity (Atomic Heart) all
   share the trait that no amount of design or business execution insulates against them — only
   jurisdiction diversification, transparency, and avoiding legally gray monetization mechanics do.

7. **A solo or small, publicly-exposed team has no buffer between a bad day and a business-ending
   decision.** Flappy Bird and Fez II are the sharpest examples: neither project died from a design
   or funding failure — both died because one individual, with no institutional shock-absorber
   around them, converted acute personal distress (guilt over addictiveness; harassment following
   a public argument) directly into an irreversible shutdown decision, within days or even hours.

8. **Discoverability, not raw quality, increasingly gates whether a good product ever finds its
   audience at all.** The indiepocalypse pattern (US/EU and China both) shows that as a storefront's
   supply grows and free discovery channels degrade, a well-made product with no marketing
   advantage increasingly fails silently — not through negative reviews, but through never being
   seen.

9. **Honest crisis communication plus real post-launch delivery is the one reliable recovery
   path.** FFXIV 1.0's reboot is the standout counter-example in this whole survey: the studio
   publicly admitted the original was unsalvageable, rebuilt from scratch rather than patching, and
   turned a genuine AAA-scale disaster into a decade-plus success. (Two other well-known cases not
   detailed above but worth naming for the same lesson: CD Projekt Red's Cyberpunk 2077 and Hello
   Games' No Man's Sky both launched to severe backlash and both recovered largely through sustained,
   transparent post-launch delivery rather than through PR alone.)

## 12. Relevance check for a project shaped like Cosmogram

Cosmogram is: a solo, non-programmer owner directing an AI coding assistant; a Telegram-native
mini-game on vanilla JS/Canvas2D with no build pipeline; monetized only through Telegram Stars
under an explicit Charter banning ads, loot boxes, and predatory selling; already carrying its own
documented scale-risk concerns (see `.knowledge/` decision registry). Sorting the above patterns by
how directly they apply to *this* project's actual shape:

### Genuinely live risks for Cosmogram

- **Platform dependency (pattern 4) is the single most structurally relevant risk in this whole
  file.** Cosmogram runs entirely inside Telegram, exactly the platform that produced the Hamster
  Kombat boom-bust — a change to Telegram Mini App policy, Stars economics, or platform rules is
  categorically the same risk class as Flash's discontinuation or Disney pulling a license, and it
  is entirely outside the owner's control. There is no design or monetization discipline that
  hedges this; only avoiding deeper platform-specific lock-in than necessary, and staying aware of
  Telegram's own policy direction, mitigates it at all.
- **Trust breaking hardest against the most loyal players (pattern 3) is directly relevant** to any
  future change to Cosmogram's Stars pricing, cosmetic economy, or "Небо благодарности" reward
  systems — any change perceived as breaking an earlier implicit promise to long-time/paying
  players carries outsized reputational risk versus the same change applied to new players.
- **Solo-team fragility under sudden exposure (pattern 7)** is relevant in spirit even though
  Cosmogram's owner is not a public-facing streamer/influencer the way Dong Nguyen or Phil Fish
  were — the underlying lesson (a single person with no institutional buffer can convert a bad
  moment into a permanent decision) generalizes to any single-owner project's resilience against
  harassment, a viral negative moment, or overwhelming unexpected growth. The project's own
  documented scale-risk concerns ("риски масштаба: миллион игроков") are already pointed at exactly
  this category.
- **Discoverability over raw quality (pattern 8)** applies at Cosmogram's actual scale: a
  well-made, ethically-monetized game inside a crowded Telegram Mini App ecosystem still has to be
  *found*, and "we didn't compromise the Charter" doesn't by itself solve distribution.
- **Honest, transparent recovery (pattern 9)** is a genuine strength already embedded in how this
  project is run (the owner is told about bugs directly, in chat, immediately — see the CLAUDE.md
  rule against burying findings in an archive) — worth naming explicitly as a pattern *to keep
  doing*, not just a risk to watch for.

### Real but lower-probability / lower-relevance risks

- **Geopolitical/sanctions risk (pattern 6)** is real for any Russian-connected developer (as the
  GSC Game World and Atomic Heart cases and the broader Russian-studio-exodus material show) but is
  largely outside the scope of code or design decisions this project's Claude Code sessions can
  influence — worth the owner's own awareness, not something to solve technically.
- **Monetization mechanics crossing into outright legal prohibition (Korea's P2E ban)** is a
  low-probability risk specifically *because* Cosmogram's Charter already rules out the exact
  category (crypto-tied play-to-earn, gambling-adjacent randomized-odds purchases) that triggered
  it — this is evidence the Charter's existing constraints are already doing real protective work,
  not a gap to close.

### Patterns that are largely irrelevant to Cosmogram's shape

- **AAA scope/budget collapse (38 Studios, Embracer, Anthem-style multi-year reboots)** — these are
  failure modes of large capital structures, publisher politics, and big teams; a solo-owner,
  no-build-pipeline vanilla-JS project has neither the capital exposure nor the organizational
  complexity that produced these failures. The closest analogue worth keeping in mind is the
  *scope-creep* half of the lesson (Star Citizen, Mighty No. 9) — not the financial-structure half.
- **Live-service genre-chasing without genre experience (Concord, Babylon's Fall, Redfall, Suicide
  Squad)** — Cosmogram is not attempting to compete in a saturated live-service PvP genre against
  incumbents; its actual competitive risk is closer to the indiepocalypse discoverability problem
  than to this pattern.
- **Crowdfunding-specific failure modes (Yogventures, Mighty No. 9's backer-trust spiral)** —
  Cosmogram is not crowdfunded and has no comparable pre-sold promise structure to break.
- **Large-conglomerate portfolio-reallocation kills (Durango, the 2024 Chinese shutdown wave)** —
  there is no parent company weighing Cosmogram against alternative uses of the same engineering
  team; the owner's own continued commitment is the only "portfolio decision" that matters here.
